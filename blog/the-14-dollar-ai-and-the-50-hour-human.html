The Economics of AI at Work, part 1

The $14 AI and the 50-hour human

October 26, 2026, 6 minute read

When a small organization considers using AI, the first question is usually "what will it cost?" It's the wrong question. The AI is almost free. The expensive part is the human work wrapped around it, and that's where the savings are.

The bill nobody sends

Take an illustrative example. A small nonprofit's shared inbox gets 1,500 emails a month. A staff member pastes each one into an AI assistant, reads the suggested category and reply, fixes it, and copies it back into email. That takes about two minutes per email.

The AI part is cheap. With instructions of about 3,000 tokens, a 300-token email and a 250-token reply, Claude Sonnet 5.5 costs about $14 a month at Anthropic's current API prices. The smaller Claude Haiku 5.5 would cost under $1.1

The human part isn't. Two minutes times 1,500 emails is 50 hours a month. At the median wage for administrative assistants, $23.23 an hour, that's about $1,162 a month.2 If volunteers do the work, it's worth even more: Independent Sector values a volunteer hour at $36.14, or about $1,807.3

Even inside the $14, most of the cost isn't the emails. About two-thirds is the instructions, which get sent again with every single email. Anthropic's prompt caching charges 5% of the normal input price when repeated instructions are reused, which would bring this example closer to $5 if most requests reuse the cached instructions.1 But whether the bill is $14 or $5 barely matters next to $1,162. There is almost no money to be saved by making the AI cheaper.

An old problem in economics

In the 1960s, economists William Baumol and William Bowen pointed out that a string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same time to play Schubert as it did a century earlier. Factories kept getting more productive, but live performance couldn't. Musicians' wages still had to rise with everyone else's, so concerts kept getting more expensive. Economists call this Baumol's cost disease. It explains why labor-heavy services like health care, education and social services keep getting costlier.4

Most nonprofits sit squarely in that group. Their work is people serving people, and their costs are mostly people's time. AI is one of the first tools that could raise productivity in that kind of work. But it only cures the disease if it changes the hours. An AI that drafts a reply in two seconds, followed by two minutes of copying and pasting, leaves the cost almost exactly where it was.

What the research finds

Studies of AI at work show real gains, but on specific tasks:

StudyWhoFinding
Noy and Zhang, Science (2023)5453 college-educated professionals doing writing tasksAbout 40% less time, and about 18% higher quality
Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2023)6Customer-support agents14% more issues resolved per hour, with the largest gains for less experienced agents
Anthropic (November 2025)7100,000 real Claude conversationsTasks about 80% faster, by Claude's own estimates

Anthropic is careful about what its 80% figure leaves out. It can't count "additional time humans spend on tasks outside of their conversations with Claude, including validating the quality or accuracy of Claude's work."7 In other words, it measures the AI part of the work, not the human part around it.

Anthropic's January 2026 report then asked what happens when you account for that. Adjusting for how often AI actually succeeds roughly halves its estimate of the boost to US productivity growth. Treating the tasks AI doesn't speed up as bottlenecks lowers it further.8

That bottleneck effect is Baumol again, inside a single job. When one part of the work gets much faster and another doesn't, the slow part sets the pace. In the inbox example, the slow part is a person copying and pasting.

Why the gains show up late

Economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson describe a productivity J-curve for technologies like AI. Getting value from them requires investing in new processes, skills and ways of working, and those investments don't show up in output at first. Productivity can look flat, or even dip, before it rises.9

For a small team, the investment is unglamorous: connecting the inbox so emails reach the AI automatically, having drafts land in the right place, and leaving people only the review. That's the step that bends the curve.

Do the math on the human, not the tokens

Back to the example. Suppose a volunteer or contractor spends 25 hours, at $50 an hour, connecting the inbox so that staff only review drafts. That's $1,250, once. Review takes about 30 seconds instead of two minutes, so the team gets back 37.5 hours a month, worth about $871 at the same wage. The AI bill doesn't change.

At 1,500 emails a month, the automation pays for itself in under two months. Even at a few hundred emails a month, it pays back within a year. The decision that matters isn't which AI model to buy. It's whether to remove the human steps around it.

Investors already price it this way

Venture investors reached the same conclusion from the other side:

  • Sell the work, not the tool. Benchmark's Sarah Tavel argues that AI startups should sell finished work, priced "relative to the cost of a human performing the work." Building a tool that only helps a person do the work, she writes, means "selling a 10% productivity improvement instead of 95%."10
  • The market is labor, not software. Foundation Capital's Ashu Garg and Jaya Gupta put the opportunity at the $4.6 trillion companies spend on salaries and services, not the roughly $200 billion software market.11
  • Human steps cost AI companies too. Andreessen Horowitz found AI companies' gross margins often run 50–60%, below the 60–80% typical of software companies, because of cloud computing costs and the cost of people kept in the loop.12

What this means for product managers

  • Measure human minutes per task, not just AI cost per task. It's usually the number that decides whether a feature pays off.
  • Design out the handoffs. Every copy, paste and app switch is a cost the user pays on every use.
  • Keep review where mistakes are expensive, and only there. Deciding where a person must stay in the loop is the subject of the next article in this series.

A checklist for small teams

  1. Count how many items a month the work involves, and time how long a person spends on each.
  2. Price the AI separately. It will usually be a small line.
  3. Find the manual steps around the AI: copying, pasting, reformatting and switching apps.
  4. Price removing them, and work out the payback period.
  5. After the change, measure the minutes again.

The question isn't what the AI costs. It's what the work around it costs, and how much of that you can give back to the people doing it.
