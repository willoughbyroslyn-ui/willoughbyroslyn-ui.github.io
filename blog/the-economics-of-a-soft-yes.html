The Economics of AI at Work, part 2

The economics of a soft yes

September 20, 2026, 5 minute read

A car dealership's chatbot agreed to sell a new SUV for one dollar. An airline's chatbot promised a refund the airline didn't offer, and a tribunal made the airline pay it. An AI running a small shop was talked into handing out discounts and free items. Each time, something speaking for an organization said yes when nobody with authority had. I call that a soft yes, and it has a price.

Three soft yeses

The three failed in different ways. Air Canada's bot stated a policy wrongly. The dealership's bot was manipulated. Anthropic's shopkeeper was simply persuaded. But the result was the same: a commitment nobody approved, made in the organization's name.

An old idea: the principal and the agent

Economists have a name for this. When one party (the principal) has another (the agent) act on its behalf, the agent knows different things and wants different things, and the gap costs money. Michael Jensen and William Meckling called these agency costs in a 1976 paper.4 Companies spend heavily to manage them, with approval limits, signing authority and audits.

An AI assistant is the most literal agent an organization has ever had. It talks to customers in the organization's name, at any hour, at any volume. And as the Air Canada ruling shows, the customer doesn't see a separate system. To them, the chatbot is the company. The tribunal found the airline "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate."2

Pricing a yes

Not every answer needs a person. Making someone check everything wastes the time AI was supposed to save. The question is where to draw the line, and there's a simple rule for it:

Let the AI say yes on its own when the chance it's wrong, times the cost if it's wrong, is less than the cost of a person checking.

Suppose a review takes a person five minutes. At Independent Sector's value of a volunteer hour, $36.14, that's about $3.5 If the AI is wrong 2% of the time, review pays for itself whenever a wrong promise would cost more than $150. If it's wrong only 0.5% of the time, the line moves to $600.

Two things follow. First, you need the error rate, and you only get it by testing. That's the subject of the next article in this series. Second, the costliest promises are worth checking even when the AI is very reliable, because a single mistake can cost more than thousands of reviews.

Who can say yes

Banks, trading desks and investment firms already work this way. Who can commit capital, sign a term sheet or exceed a risk limit is written down, and the riskier the action, the fewer people can take it. An AI assistant needs the same map, and it needs it written down before launch, not after the first bad promise.

When someone talks the AI into yes

The dealership case is a warning about manipulation. If an AI's authority lives only in its instructions, a clever message can talk it out of them. Programmer and writer Simon Willison describes a "lethal trifecta" for AI agents: access to private data, exposure to content an attacker could write, and a way to send information out. His advice is to never combine all three.6

The same logic applies to promises. An AI that reads messages from the public shouldn't be able to commit money, stock or dates by itself. Put that authority in the system, as an approval step a person must click, rather than in a sentence of instructions asking the AI to be careful.

What Anthropic recommends

Anthropic's framework for safe agents says "humans should retain control over how their goals are pursued, particularly before high-stakes decisions are made." Its own example is an agent that finds wasteful software subscriptions: before it cancels any, "the company would likely want a human to give approval." Claude Code works the same way. It can read files without asking but "must ask for human approval before taking any actions that modify code or systems."7 Anthropic's guide to building agents adds that they can "pause for human feedback at checkpoints" and recommends "extensive testing in sandboxed environments, along with the appropriate guardrails."8

What this means for product managers

  • List every promise the product can make: prices, dates, refunds, availability, eligibility. If it isn't on the list, the AI shouldn't make it.
  • Assign each promise to a square on the grid, and build the permission into the product rather than the prompt.
  • Make approval fast. If approving takes longer than doing the work, people will route around it.
  • Log every yes. When something goes wrong, you'll want to know who or what said it, and why.

A checklist for small teams

  1. Write down everything your AI assistant could promise.
  2. For each, estimate what a wrong promise would cost and whether it can be undone.
  3. Place each on the grid: AI answers, AI answers from approved text, AI drafts and a person approves, or only a person decides.
  4. Write a short "never promise" list into the assistant's instructions and into staff training.
  5. Test how often the AI gets each kind of answer wrong, and move the lines as you learn.

A soft yes feels free when it's made. The cost arrives later, in a refund, a tribunal ruling or a tungsten cube given away. Deciding in advance who can say yes is one of the cheapest things an organization can do.
